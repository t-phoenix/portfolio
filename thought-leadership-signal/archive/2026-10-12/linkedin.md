## Post

A five year vote cannot govern a country that updates every hour.

On 10 October, youth organisers under the Cockroach Janta Party banner called for Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar to resign over the special intensive revision of voter rolls. They planned to gather at Jantar Mantar. @Reuters and @AFP showed what arrived first: barricades in Delhi, detentions in multiple cities, leaders held on arrival, a public argument treated as a security event before it could become a deliberation.

I am not posting this as a party brief. I am posting it as a systems receipt. When media, courts, election machinery, and law and order feel colluded into one story, trust does not vanish in the survey. It shrinks into tribes.

The 2026 @Edelman Trust Barometer is useful here because it refuses the easy story. India still ranks high on government trust among the markets they measure. The quieter finding is insularity. Nearly half prefer people who think like them, consume the same media, or share their values. There is also a wide trust gap between higher and lower income groups. High trust and brittle trust can live in the same country.

That is a design problem, not only a moral one.

We still treat elections as the main repair loop. Four or five years. One day. Then silence until the next ceremony. A generation that lives in continuous feeds will not experience that as accountability. They experience it as a system that only listens when it is forced to.

There are working case studies, imperfect and real.

vTaiwan, with tools like Pol.is, built a continuous public deliberation path that helped shape about 26 digital economy related laws. People mapped agreement and disagreement in public, then government officers had to engage the process.

@e-Estonia built X Road so ministries and services exchange data with authentication, encryption, access rules, and logs. Trust became an infrastructure property: you can ask who queried what, under which rule.

On chain governance is not a national constitution. It is a laboratory. In October 2024, Andrew Hall and Sho Miyazaki at Stanford GSB published a study of liquid democracy across 18 @Ethereum based DAOs. Over 250,000 voters. About 1,700 proposals. On average about 17% of voting tokens were delegated. Smaller holders delegated upward. Active voters attracted more weight. When projects built hubs to coordinate delegation, both delegation and voting rose. The paper is honest about the failure mode too. Participation stays low among both voters and delegates. New tools do not magically create civic energy.

So the case is not "install blockchain, fix India."

The case is this.

Governance for a digital society needs three layers at once:

1. A continuous feedback path between elections, so anger has a legitimate channel before it becomes street force.
2. Public audit trails for decisions and data access, so opacity is expensive.
3. Delegation with skin in the game, so specialists can carry weight without erasing the person's ability to take the vote back.

Technology can host those layers. It cannot replace courage in courts, press, and election officers. But ceremonies alone are obsolete for the speed at which people now live.

Where does your city or company still run on a ceremony, when what you need is a continuous audit?
