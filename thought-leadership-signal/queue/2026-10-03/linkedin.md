## Post

They let a model trade every 300 milliseconds. A simple rule still won.

Jev is fast enough that people skipped the awkward middle step, which is measuring anything. The pattern in public repos is easy to recognize. Feed a price. Ask buy, sell, or hold. Let software send the order. That demo feels like a product because money appears to move.

A connection is the easy part. A result is not.

Someone sat down with 19 public finance projects built around Jev, in the week of September 24. Most were demos or paper trading. Four of them ran the honest test: Jev next to a simpler rule. In all four, the rule won or tied. That is not a scandal. It is what you should expect from a model that was never trained to be a fund.

A simulated desk makes the cost obvious. About 731 trades in a day. Roughly 3,150 dollars lost. About 1,650 dollars in fees. About 21 percent of trades green after costs. The author even told the model that fees exist. Fees still ate the clever part.

So I unglue three claims before I get impressed.

It answers fast. That is latency.

It can send an order. That is plumbing.

It makes money after costs. That is the only sentence that counts as an edge.

If you only have the first two, you have a toy that looks like a desk.

What did you measure the last time a bot looked alive?
